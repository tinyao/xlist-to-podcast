text: My biggest takeaways from expert comp negotiator Jacob Warwick:
  
  1. Product leaders, engineers, and designers often negotiate worse than louder functions. Jacob’s observation is that thoughtful, introverted, highly competent people tend to over-index on being reasonable and under-index on testing the ceiling. Meanwhile, more extroverted functions are often more comfortable treating negotiation as part of the job. That was a useful distinction because it explains why some of the people creating the most value inside companies can still under-negotiate relative to peers who are simply more willing to ask.
  
  2. A very soft pushback can materially change your offer. Jacob’s simplest line is “What’s the chance there’s a little more here?” He says he often sees roughly 20% movement from that alone, across everything from earlier-stage roles to seven-figure roles. What makes this useful is that it removes the fantasy people have about negotiation needing to sound aggressive or manipulative. His whole point is that the first step is often just opening the conversation in a calm, low-friction way.
  
  3. Jacob typically looks for around 40% movement when a negotiation is run well. That doesn’t mean every offer magically jumps 40%. It means most candidates are negotiating far too narrowly because they’re reacting to the first number, the first band, or the first title they hear instead of building leverage throughout the process. One of the clearest messages in the episode is that negotiation does not start when the offer lands. It starts much earlier, with how much information you collect, how well you understand the company’s real needs, and how strongly you position yourself as the solution to their biggest challenges.
  
  4. Great negotiation is collaborative, not adversarial. This came up repeatedly, and it is probably the biggest misconception the episode corrects. Jacob is not teaching people to beat companies up for the sake of it. He is teaching them to better understand the value at stake and structure a deal that reflects it, sometimes through salary, sometimes through equity, and sometimes through more creative mechanisms like milestone-based compensation. His framing was essentially: don’t just fight over the pie; find ways to expand it.
  
  5. The path to wealth is equity, not just W-2 comp. Jacob is very clear that salary matters, but he thinks many candidates focus too narrowly on base pay and not enough on equity, milestone triggers, performance-based upside, and other ways of participating in the value they help create. This was also where his founder perspective came through most clearly. The best negotiation is not necessarily the one with the biggest base; it’s the one that best matches how much upside you can unlock.
  
  6. Your first comp number often becomes your ceiling. This may have been the most useful tactical point in the whole conversation. The danger of naming your number too early is that you are anchoring before you understand the actual scope of the job. By the time you’ve gone through the interview loop, you may realize the company is really hiring for a much bigger role than the original job description suggested. But if you already gave a number, that old number can get used against you.
  
  7. Information + timing create power. Jacob says this explicitly, and it seems to be the backbone of his whole system. Slow the process down. Ask better questions. Learn more before you reveal too much. Create more surface area for understanding what the company needs, who the internal champions are, and where the actual constraints sit. The more rushed you feel, the weaker your position usually is.
  
  8. You should almost never split the difference. Jacob sees this as one of the laziest habits in negotiation because it creates the illusion of progress without telling you whether you’ve actually found the ceiling. Just because a company moved does not mean the deal was negotiated well. A related point he makes is that short, confident communication often works better than over-explaining. Sometimes the move is not a giant speech. Sometimes it is simply: “Open to chat?”

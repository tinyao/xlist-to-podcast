text: My biggest takeaways from @tdrobbo (CPO at @Whatnot):
  
  1. AI’s biggest unlock for PMs now is in data science (not prototyping). Cohort retention pulls, analyzing user logs, and sensitivity and regression models on a @_hex_tech thread—work that took an Amazon L7 data scientist a week or two in 2017 can be done in minutes. Tom spends 10x more time in data than at any point in his career and less time talking to data scientists than ever.
  
  2. Tom “regrets that product management exists,” but this isn't an insult to PMs. Tom argues that PM is a trade, not a qualification—a muscle built by reps. Hiring too many PMs as a default takes reps away from engineers and designers, infantilizing people perfectly capable of good decisions who never had to make them because “there was always a PM to babysit them.”
  
  3. AI is exposing rampant “product theater.” Stakeholder management, alignment, storytelling—real work, but not product work, and they’re what the industry has rewarded PMs for the past five years.   4. The PM skill trending up fastest: systems thinking. Tom asks this question in every product review: “What do we do if the experiment is green? What do we do if it’s red?” If the answer is “I don’t know,” you haven't done the mental work necessary to run this experiment.
  
  5. Stop promoting your A-players out of the work they’re great at. The common trap: take an A-player PM, make them a director, tell them to stop being hands-on, watch their instincts atrophy while junior PMs yo-yo the same PRD through review. At Whatnot, the four or five PM managers each spend at least 90% of their time on IC work; Tom spends about half his time as CPO as an IC. 
  
  6. “It’s really complex” is usually code for weak leadership. Twitter ran working groups on lifting the 140-character limit for Tom’s entire tenure. Everyone knew it was inevitable—Japanese users tweeted six times as often because kanji packs more meaning per character—but nobody would make the call. It shipped almost two years after he left, and nothing bad happened. When something seems complicated, ask whether it’s actually complicated or whether leadership just won’t decide.
  
  7. Map PMs to problems, not teams. Every six months, Tom, the CEO, and senior leads define the company strategy and key pillars, then assign DRIs by skill set. Some engineering teams go a year without a PM. The result: a PM who’s worked on buyer, seller, and trust problems instead of spending three years on notifications. More reps, more muscle groups.
  
  8. Averages mean nothing to the individual. This is the thread running through Tom’s worst decisions. A feature only 3% of users touch looks like a deprecation candidate—until you go a layer deeper and find that for some users it’s 100% of their use case, and killing it is like “a Westfield mall turning off the power in the lead-up to Christmas.” Always ask what distribution hides under the average, and when the anecdote conflicts with the data, trust the anecdote.
  
  9. True PMF is something you feel before you see it in the data. Twitter had nine heads of product in the two years Tom was there and survived anyway, because the product had a level of PMF that absorbed extraordinary mismanagement. Elon changed the name, brand, team, and culture; the network effects held. The career lesson: work somewhere with true PMF early so you learn what bottled lightning actually feels like.
  
  10. End turf wars by giving one person both sides of the tradeoff. At Twitch, discovery and ads were perpetually at war for impressions. One of Tom’s first moves was to put ads inside discovery under a single PM accountable for total GMV from the feed, organic and paid alike. The tradeoff gets made naturally, cutting months of back-and-forth and the politics that turn decisions from company-first to career-first.
